When the renown of Bahram (Gur) as king had risen from the Eish up to the moon,

The hearts of the distinguished gained new strength; the fame of (all) the famous lived again.

]VIalignants perished in obscurity, the heads of all beneath black water plunged.

There was a good old man, Narsi byname — (the same name had the brother of Bahram) —

His judgment strong, his reason perfect too, he knew of things before (they had occurred).

His lineage was that of King Dara — this was no secret, but a well-known thing.

The king would never be apart from him; he was both his companion and vazir.

He had three sons, each one of whom possessed a certain branch of knowledge to himself.

(Then) he who was the eldest of the sons Zaravand by his father had been named.

The monarch had discerned his standard worth, and he had made him chief of aU his priests.

He thought as gnostics do, he knew the Way, and measureless was his asceticism.

The second was collector-general, impost-receiver upon all the roads.

Through the integrity of his control the king had given him power throughout the land.

The third was in the business of the town and army the king’s special deputy.

The monarch left the government to them; the governors were faithful to their trust.

Each night he brightened up the feast with wine; each day his governors were at their work.

Eevolving like a mill around Mmself, Trhatever lie acquired lie threw away.

This story was divulged throughout the world; axes were sharpened with a view to cut.

All said, Bahram is drunk, and bas exchanged religion for the world, and sword for cup.

With boon companions he is lost in wine; (all) he enjoys (is) wine, his gain’s the wind.

The mind of each was shirred with the desire that the realm’s business should be ruled by him.

The khan of khans from China (then) set out to take the monarch of the world’s abode.

With bim, like formidable dragons, were three hundred thousand archers strong and skilled.

He crossed the Oxus and advanced with speed; a resurrection in Khurasan raised.

(And) from the king’s viceroys he seized by force of Transoxiana all the (broad) domains.

The king got information of the raid, but in his army had no confidence.

He saw that all by pleasure’s hand were nursed, (and) from war’s ways that they withheld their hands.

(That) those who were the leaders of the troops were (nowise) single-hearted towards the king.

Each one sent out an envoy in advance with letters to the khaqan on their views.

(Each one) conceived ill-feeling for his king; thought (but) of saving his own wealth and land.

They said (unto the khan), We’re all your friends; pursue the road, we’re dust upon your road.

You are the monarch of the world, advance, come on, for Bahram cannot act as king.

If you desire we’ll use the sword to him; and if not, we will bind and give him up.

A scribe, as one who’s competent to read the letters (that he writes), informed the king.

Tile king abandoned hope of Persian faith, (and) left the kingdom to (his) lieutenants.

He went himself and hid his face (from view), (for) with such instruments one cannot fight.

The world’s king — so it flashed throughout the world — had hid his face from kingdom and from troops.

Unable with the khaqan and his troops to cope, he’d fled disordered from (the scene).

The messenger brought greeting to the khan (advising him) the king had left the throne;

Adding, You’re favoured with the crown and belt; advance, (for) neither crown nor throne remains.

After the khan had heard the message brought, that Bahram (Gut) had vanished from the world;

That he from sword and sword-play held his hand, (and) sat down carelessly to lute and wine;

That, troubled not for foe, he drank of wine, and did such things as should not have been done.

He did what in his foe he’d not approved, so that his foe (thereafter) laughed at him.

King Bahram day and night was at the chase, whilst couriers in (his) business were engaged.

About the Chinese leader news he sought, imtil his courier brought him truthful news:

That he felt safe, at ease about the king — this was of happiest omen to the king.

When he prepared, of all those troops of his there were three hundred horsemen, none besides.

Each one had seen, (each one) was tried in war; dragons on land, on water, crocodiles.

Of one heart all as pomegranate with grains: though hundred-grained, of one receptacle.

The king used stratagem against his foe, (against him) plotted all in secrecy.

His enemy sought fire, he gave him smoke; inspired him with false confidence, and soon

He amed (his) shaft well at the mark, his (foe), for he knew well how he was circumstanced.

He made a sudden night-attack on him; he raised the dust above the seven skies.

On a dark night, which, in its black control, proceeded as a black snake with the founts —

A night which had removed (all) lamps away, (when) mount and plain were blacker than crows’ plumes;

(When) countless drunken Ethiops, as it were, were running sword in hand from side to side;

(And) fearing those Ethiops who ran about, men opened wide their eyes, though naught they saw;

(When) the bright-hearted sky in black sHk dressed was like a jar of gold closed up with pitch —

On such a night of ambergris so pure Bahram (went out and) waged Bahramian war.

(And) so he rushed upon the valorous, attacking now with sword and now with shaft.

The arrow which he shot at any place became free in a moment from the mark,

The eye of caution of his foemen slept before his arrow, which could pierce hard stone.

They saw the wound, the arrow was not seen; the arrow seen, the wound was not there (near).

They all said. What contrivance should this be? — The shafts remote from wounds, the wounds from shafts!

Until it came to this that no one (dared) within a league of his arena come.

He rushed on all sides like a cloud; through him the plain became a mount, the mount a plain.

He killed so many of those troops with shafts that with the blood the earth grew soft as paste.

The frame of whomsoe’er his arrow hit — the soul at once departed from that frame.

When dawn drew forth the falchion of the sun, and on the sky a bowl of blood appeared,

Prom all the blood shed by the hero-king a stream of blood flowed, bore down beads as balls.

And through the numbers slaughtered by the sword the gall-bladder was shirred to vomit bile.

The lance (there) wagered with the sword its bead that dragons it would reap like ears of corn.

The shaft was in the fight a darting snake — when darts the snake ’his bad (for all who’re near).

King Babram fighting in the battle ranks, bis arrow-points-as hairs, were splitting hairs.

If with bis sword be struck a horseman’s bead, down to the waist be clove him like a gourd.

And if with stroke oblique be terrificd, be cleft the man asunder at the waist.

Of this kind was (bis) sword, of that, (bis) shaft — ’his likely that the foe would be dismayed.

The Turks from this bis sudden Turk-like raid, and wounds so deadly on the path be took,

Inclined to flight; the swords of all of them became (all) blunted, and their racing keen.

When the king’s sword was brandished on all sides, the Turkish troops relaxed in (their) attempts.

The king discerning signs of victory, drove (at the foe bis) sword, and shot (bis) shafts.

By the shock of (bis) sword be broke their ranks: be was the wind, you’d say, and they were clouds.

In triumph to his troops he called (aloud), Behold (our) fortune, see (our happy) lot!

That we may strike a bead off strive again, that we may tear the centre from its place.

Supporting one another (then) they charged, lions beneath them, dragons in their bands.

The right retired, the left wing fled away, the centre poured into the vanguard’s rear.

The king obtained a bold on victory: be routed all who at the centre (fought).

An army more in number than the grains of sand and earth he ruined by his raids.

The hardness of the swarthy lions’ claws pounded the brains of those whose swords were soft.

Eapid in action as the snake (their) shafts; from their effects the horsemen fell and lay.

Through the sharp dagger’s (work) the dust of flight reached the Turks’ army to the Oxus stream.

The king such store of gems and treasure took that treasurers were troubled in the count.

Returning from that conquest to his realm, he showed for (all his) people kindly care.

In triumph then ascending to the throne, he garbed the world afresh in New Year’s joys.

All swept the ground before him (with their brows) and, suited to (his) conquest, gave him praise.

Singers in Pahlavi, with Persian times sang to the harp’s sweet sounds in Pahlavi.

The Arab poets to the rebeck’s tones recited verses like pellucid pearls.

The king, a judge of skill, a connoisseur in poetry, gave them unmeasured wealth.

Prom that great spoil and treasure he endowed the temples with a thousand camel (loads).

In skirtfuls gold, and pearls in hatfuls too, he to the priesthood of the temples gave.

So much gold from his treasury he gave that no one poor remained in (aU) the world.
